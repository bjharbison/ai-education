Chapter 01
Theory

What Is a Semiconductor?

The word "semiconductor" describes a material that conducts electricity better than an insulator like glass but worse than a conductor like copper. That unremarkable middle ground turns out to be the most commercially valuable property in the history of engineering — because it can be precisely controlled. A semiconductor's conductivity can be tuned across twelve orders of magnitude by the application of an electric field, a tiny voltage, or the introduction of trace atomic impurities. This controllability is the foundation of every transistor, and therefore every digital device on Earth.

Band Theory — Why Materials Conduct at All

To understand why silicon behaves the way it does, you need the quantum mechanical picture of solids. When individual atoms form a crystal lattice, their discrete electron energy levels broaden into continuous bands separated by gaps. The two critical bands are the valence band — the highest band fully populated with electrons at absolute zero — and the conduction band above it, which is empty at zero temperature.

The bandgap is the energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band. Its size determines everything about a material's electrical character:

At room temperature (kT ≈ 0.026 eV), silicon has just enough thermal energy to excite a small number of electrons across its 1.12 eV gap, creating electron-hole pairs in a process called intrinsic conduction. This gives pure silicon a resistivity of about 2,300 Ω·cm — useful, but not yet controllable enough to make a transistor.

Doping — Precision Impurity Engineering

Doping is the deliberate introduction of trace quantities of impurity atoms into a semiconductor crystal to dramatically alter its electrical properties. The precision required is staggering: one phosphorus atom per 10 million silicon atoms is a "heavily doped" semiconductor. This is equivalent to adding one drop of food colouring to an Olympic swimming pool and expecting a consistent, measurable colour change everywhere.

N-type doping introduces atoms with one more valence electron than silicon — typically phosphorus or arsenic. The extra electron is weakly bound and easily promoted to the conduction band at room temperature, donating a free negative charge carrier. The dopant atoms are called donors.

P-type doping introduces atoms with one fewer valence electron — boron is the most common. Each boron atom creates a missing electron, or hole — a positively charged vacancy that moves through the crystal as surrounding electrons fill it. Boron atoms are called acceptors.

Carrier concentration after doping: n · p = nᵢ² (mass action law — always true at equilibrium) nᵢ ≈ 1.5 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ for silicon at 300K N-type: n ≈ Nᴅ (donor concentration), p = nᵢ²/Nᴅ ≪ n P-type: p ≈ Nᴬ (acceptor concentration), n = nᵢ²/Nᴬ ≪ p Typical Nᴅ or Nᴬ: 10¹⁵ – 10²⁰ cm⁻³ Doping ratio: 10⁵ – 10¹⁰ × the intrinsic carrier concentration

The pn Junction — Nature's Switch

Join a p-type region and an n-type region at a single interface and something remarkable happens spontaneously: electrons from the n-side and holes from the p-side diffuse across the boundary and recombine, creating a depletion region — a zone stripped of free carriers, carrying a built-in electric field pointing from n to p. This field opposes further diffusion and establishes an equilibrium.

Apply a forward voltage (p-side positive) and you reduce the built-in field, allowing carriers to flow — current increases exponentially with voltage. Apply a reverse voltage and you widen the depletion region, increasing its resistance and blocking current. This asymmetric current-voltage relationship is the diode — the simplest controllable electronic device, and the building block of every transistor.

Case Study — Why Silicon Defeated Germanium

The first transistors (1947, Bell Labs) used germanium, not silicon. Germanium was easier to purify at the time, had a smaller bandgap (0.67 eV) requiring lower voltages, and was better understood. Through the early 1950s, germanium dominated commercial transistor production.

Silicon won for three reasons that only became apparent over time:

1. Native oxide. Silicon forms a stable, dense, electrically insulating oxide (SiO₂) naturally when exposed to oxygen at elevated temperature. This native oxide became the gate dielectric of the MOSFET and the isolation between transistors — enabling billions of devices on one chip. Germanium's native oxide (GeO₂) dissolves in water and is mechanically unstable. There is no equivalent "germanium gate" technology that ever worked reliably at scale.

2. Thermal stability. Silicon's 1.12 eV bandgap means its intrinsic carrier concentration remains low until ~150°C. Germanium's 0.67 eV gap causes intrinsic carriers to overwhelm intentional doping above ~75°C. As transistors got smaller and more densely packed, junction temperatures rose. Silicon devices kept working; germanium devices didn't.

3. Abundance and cost. Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust (27.7% by mass). Germanium is a trace element, typically extracted as a byproduct of zinc smelting. As production volumes scaled from thousands to billions of transistors, the economics became decisive.

Case Study — When Silicon Isn't Enough: GaAs in RF

Gallium arsenide (GaAs) has been the material of choice for radio-frequency (RF) amplifiers and wireless chips since the 1970s — despite being far more expensive and difficult to manufacture than silicon. Why?

Electron mobility. In GaAs, electrons move roughly 5–6× faster than in silicon at the same electric field (8,500 vs 1,400 cm²/V·s). Faster electrons mean higher operating frequencies — GaAs transistors can amplify signals at tens of GHz where silicon struggles. Your phone's cellular modem uses a GaAs power amplifier in the transmit chain. So does every 5G mmWave device.

Direct bandgap. GaAs has a direct bandgap, meaning electrons can recombine with holes by emitting a photon efficiently. Silicon's indirect bandgap makes it a poor light emitter. This is why LEDs, laser diodes, and solar cells use GaAs and its compound cousins (InGaAs, AlGaAs, InP) rather than silicon.

The limit: GaAs cannot be scaled to the densities silicon achieves. There is no stable native oxide for GaAs, making dense CMOS logic impossible. GaAs remains confined to high-frequency and optoelectronic applications — a niche that is enormously valuable but structurally limited in scope.

Focus Areas for Future Study

  • Wide-bandgap semiconductors (GaN, SiC): Dominating power electronics for EVs and renewable energy. GaN is also replacing GaAs in some RF applications. Understanding their physics opens the power electronics market — entirely separate from digital computing but enormous in scale.
  • 2D materials: Graphene, MoS₂, WSe₂. At 1–2 atoms thick, they are the thinnest possible semiconductor. IBM and others are actively researching 2D channel transistors as a post-silicon candidate for sub-1nm nodes. No commercial products yet, but the physics literature is rich.
  • Organic semiconductors: Flexible, printable electronics. Already in OLED displays; the theory of charge transport in disordered organic solids is a distinct branch of physics from crystalline semiconductor theory.
  • Key text: Physics of Semiconductor Devices by Sze and Ng — the canonical graduate reference. For intuition first, Neamen's Semiconductor Physics and Devices is more accessible.

Theory + Case Study

From Atom to Transistor

The transistor is the most manufactured object in history. In 2024, the global semiconductor industry produced roughly 10²² transistors — more than the number of stars in the observable universe, more than the number of grains of sand on Earth, and more than a billion per human alive. Each one is a pn junction junction engineered with atomic precision, switching billions of times per second.

The MOSFET — How a Voltage Controls a Current

The MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) is the dominant transistor type in digital logic. Its operating principle exploits the ability of an electric field, applied perpendicular to the silicon surface through a thin insulating oxide, to create or destroy a conducting channel.

An N-channel MOSFET has a p-type silicon body with two heavily doped n-type regions called the source and drain. Under normal circumstances (no gate voltage), the two pn junctions back-to-back block current flow. When a positive voltage exceeding the threshold voltage Vₜ is applied to the gate electrode, the electric field inverts the p-type region beneath the oxide, creating a thin n-type channel — an inversion layer — through which electrons can flow from source to drain.

MOSFET drain current (simplified long-channel model): Linear regime (Vds < Vgs – Vt): Id = μₙ·Cox·(W/L)·[(Vgs – Vt)·Vds – Vds²/2] Saturation regime (Vds ≥ Vgs – Vt): Id = ½·μₙ·Cox·(W/L)·(Vgs – Vt)² Where: μₙ = electron mobility (cm²/V·s) Cox = gate oxide capacitance per area (F/cm²) W = channel width L = channel length (the critical scaling parameter) Vgs = gate-to-source voltage Vt = threshold voltage

The profound insight from this equation is the W/L ratio. To increase current (drive strength), you can widen the channel (↑W) or shorten it (↓L). Making L shorter is the goal of every process node shrink — a 10% reduction in L gives a 10% increase in current at the same power, or equivalently, the same current at 10% less voltage (which reduces power quadratically). This is the physics behind Moore's Law.

Dennard Scaling — The Golden Era and Its End

Dennard scaling, described by Robert Dennard at IBM in 1974, was the elegant physical relationship that made Moore's Law economically irresistible for three decades. Dennard observed that if you scaled all transistor dimensions (L, W, oxide thickness) and supply voltage by a factor 1/κ simultaneously, you got:

Dennard scaling consequences (shrink by 1/κ): Transistor area: 1/κ² (density doubles every shrink) Switching delay: 1/κ (faster by κ) Dynamic power per tx: 1/κ² (energy per switch drops) Power density: constant (same watts/cm²) ∴ Each generation: 2× transistors, same chip area, same power per area, but κ× faster. This held from ~1974 to ~2004.

Dennard scaling broke in the early 2000s for two related reasons. First, leakage current — current that flows even when the transistor is "off" — stopped scaling down as fast as active current. As oxide thickness approached a few nanometres, quantum mechanical tunnelling allowed electrons to pass directly through the gate dielectric even with no applied voltage. Power spent on leakage doesn't do useful computation; it just heats the chip. Second, scaling supply voltage below ~1V became impractical because Vₜ must remain a reasonable fraction of Vdd, and reducing Vₜ exponentially increases leakage.

The practical consequence: from ~2004 onward, chip designers could still pack more transistors per millimetre (density scaling continued), but the per-transistor power benefit largely disappeared. This is why CPU clock speeds plateaued around 3–4 GHz in 2005 and haven't significantly increased since — the power density would become unmanageable. The industry pivoted to multi-core architectures, trading single-thread speed for parallelism, and eventually to domain-specific accelerators optimised for specific workloads (GPUs, TPUs, NPUs).

The FinFET Revolution

By the Intel 22nm node (2011), the planar MOSFET was running out of headroom. As channel length shrank below ~25nm, the drain electric field began to dominate the channel electrostatics more than the gate field — a phenomenon called short-channel effects. The gate lost control. Leakage spiked. The transistor stopped being a good switch.

The solution, commercialised by Intel and quickly adopted by TSMC and Samsung, was the FinFET (Fin Field-Effect Transistor). Instead of a flat planar channel, the silicon channel is sculpted into a vertical thin fin, and the gate wraps around three sides of it. This "wrap-around gate" geometry dramatically improves electrostatic control — the gate field reaches into the channel from multiple directions, restoring gate dominance over the drain even at very short channel lengths.

FinFETs enabled continued scaling through Intel 22nm, 14nm, 10nm, TSMC's N7, N5, N3 nodes. But fins have their own scaling limit: as the fin width narrows below ~5nm to maintain electrostatic control at short channel lengths, variability in fin width (caused by atomic-scale roughness in the etch process) introduces unacceptable threshold voltage variation across a chip.

Gate-All-Around — The Present Frontier

Gate-All-Around (GAA) transistors, commercialised by Samsung in their 3GAE node (2022) and by TSMC in N2 (2024/25), extend FinFET logic by replacing the vertical fin with horizontal nanosheets — thin slabs of silicon stacked vertically, each surrounded on all four sides by gate material. This "nanosheet" or "MBCFET" geometry provides the ultimate electrostatic control: maximum gate area relative to channel volume.

Case Study — Intel's 10nm Delay and What It Taught the Industry

Intel announced its 10nm process in 2015 and promised volume production by 2016. Volume production eventually arrived in 2019 — three years late — after multiple delays and public write-downs. In the interim, TSMC shipped 10nm in 2016, 7nm in 2018, and 5nm in 2020, delivering Apple's A12, A13, and A14 chips. NVIDIA and AMD moved their leading products to TSMC. Intel lost its manufacturing lead for the first time since the 1980s.

The technical story is more nuanced than "Intel fell behind." Intel's 10nm targets were more aggressive than TSMC's 10nm by almost every metric — higher transistor density, tighter design rules — and the company simply underestimated the yield engineering time required to meet them. A process node is not a single parameter; it's the intersection of thousands of process steps that must all work correctly simultaneously. Each new rule added to improve density constrains the adjacent rules, creating a combinatorial explosion of interactions to characterise.

The business lesson is that semiconductor process development is not primarily a physics problem — it is a statistical manufacturing problem. TSMC's success came from a relentless focus on yield learning rate: the speed at which defects are identified, root-caused, and eliminated. By manufacturing chips for dozens of customers simultaneously at each node, TSMC accumulated more yield learning data per unit time than Intel, which primarily manufactured its own designs. Volume and diversity of customers became a competitive moat.

For your study: Intel's travails with 10nm, 7nm renaming, and the eventual Intel 4 rebranding are a masterclass in how technical debt accumulates in process development and how naming conventions in the industry became completely decoupled from physical transistor dimensions after ~2013.

Focus Areas for Future Study

  • Short-channel effects in depth: DIBL (Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering), subthreshold swing, velocity saturation — the physical mechanisms behind why transistors become hard to scale. Understanding these is prerequisite for evaluating any "post-silicon" transistor claim.
  • Process variability and statistical design: At 5nm and below, a single-atom difference in gate length between two nominally identical transistors is a ~1% variation. Statistical circuit design (Monte Carlo analysis, corner models, yield-aware sizing) is increasingly the dominant skill differentiator.
  • CFET (Complementary FET): The likely successor to GAA — stacking n-type and p-type nanosheets directly on top of each other to halve the cell footprint. Being developed for ~1nm era nodes.
  • Key papers: Dennard's 1974 paper "Design of ion-implanted MOSFETs with very small physical dimensions" (freely available). Hu's FinFET paper (2002). IEDM conference proceedings for state-of-the-art transistor announcements.

Making Chips — The Fab as a System

A semiconductor fabrication facility — a "fab" — is the most complex manufacturing environment ever built. A leading-edge fab operates at Class 1 cleanliness (fewer than 1 particle per cubic foot of air, versus Class 10,000 for a hospital operating room), maintains temperature control to ±0.01°C across the floor, and requires vibration isolation systems that can detect footsteps from 200 metres away. Understanding why reveals deep truths about the physical limits of patterning matter at the nanometre scale.

Lithography — Writing Patterns with Light

Lithography is the process of optically transferring a circuit pattern from a mask (reticle) onto a layer of light-sensitive polymer (photoresist) coated on the wafer. It is repeated 80–100 times to build up the complete device stack. The minimum feature size achievable is governed by the Rayleigh criterion:

Rayleigh criterion for minimum feature size: R = k₁ · λ / NA Where: k₁ = process factor (theoretical min 0.25, practical ~0.3–0.4) λ = illumination wavelength NA = numerical aperture of the lens system (sin of half-angle) To print smaller features: reduce λ, increase NA, reduce k₁ Historical progression: 1980s: 436nm (mercury g-line), NA ~0.3, features ~500nm 2000s: 248nm (KrF laser), NA ~0.6, features ~150nm 2010s: 193nm (ArF laser), NA 0.93 (dry) → 1.35 (immersion) 2020s: 13.5nm (EUV), NA 0.33 → 0.55 (High-NA EUV) ArF immersion at 193nm with k₁=0.35, NA=1.35: R = 0.35 × 193nm / 1.35 ≈ 50nm single exposure But with multiple patterning: effective ~12–16nm

The key insight is that multiple patterning allowed the industry to extend 193nm ArF lithography far below its single-exposure Rayleigh limit. By exposing the same layer two, three, or four times with slightly shifted or complementary masks — a technique called LELE (Litho-Etch-Litho-Etch) or SADP (Self-Aligned Double Patterning) — features smaller than the optical limit can be achieved. TSMC's N7 was manufactured entirely with ArF immersion + multiple patterning, with no EUV at all.

EUV (Extreme Ultraviolet) lithography uses 13.5nm wavelength light generated by firing a high-power CO₂ laser at tin droplets in vacuum, creating a plasma that emits EUV radiation. ASML is the sole manufacturer of EUV tools worldwide. Each machine costs approximately $150–380M (High-NA EUV is on the higher end), weighs 180 tonnes, requires 250 crates to ship, and contains over 100,000 parts. The EUV source itself generates light at less than 10% efficiency — most of the laser energy becomes heat that must be managed.

Why ASML Has a Global Monopoly on EUV

EUV lithography requires the simultaneous mastery of: high-power CO₂ laser physics, plasma physics, precision tin droplet generation (50,000 droplets/second with 20 µm diameter accuracy), reflective optics coated with alternating layers of Mo and Si at 2.8nm spacing, ultra-high vacuum systems larger than a bus, vibration isolation at sub-nanometre precision, and wafer stage positioning to ±0.15nm repeatability. No single country or company has all of these specialisations in-house. ASML's EUV machine integrates subsystems from Zeiss (optics, Germany), Trumpf (CO₂ laser, Germany), Cymer (light source, US), and hundreds of other specialist suppliers. It is arguably the most complex machine ever mass-manufactured.

The Interconnect Problem — Wires Are the Bottleneck

By the late 1990s, the transistors themselves were no longer the performance bottleneck in most chips. The wires connecting them — the BEOL metal interconnect stack — had become the limiting factor. This is the interconnect problem, and it drives several of the most important materials and architectural choices in modern semiconductors.

A metal wire has resistance R = ρL/A (where ρ is resistivity, L is length, A is cross-section). As wires scale down, A decreases, R increases. The wire also has parasitic capacitance C to adjacent wires and layers. The resulting RC delay — the time constant τ = RC for the wire to charge — increases as dimensions shrink, even as transistor switching speed improves. In modern chips, the delay through the 15–20 metal layers of BEOL interconnect often exceeds the delay through the transistors themselves.

Two materials revolutions addressed this: switching from aluminium to copper BEOL (IBM, 1997) halved wiring resistivity (ρ_Cu ≈ 1.7 vs ρ_Al ≈ 2.7 µΩ·cm), and switching from SiO₂ to low-k dielectrics (materials with dielectric constant k < 3.9) reduced parasitic capacitance. Today's advanced nodes use ultra-low-k (ULK) porous dielectrics with k ≈ 2.0–2.4, but these are mechanically fragile — the porosity that reduces k also reduces Young's modulus, creating reliability challenges during CMP and packaging.

Yield — The Economics of Imperfection

Yield in semiconductor manufacturing is the fraction of chips on a wafer that pass final test. A new process node might start at 10–20% yield and ramp to 80–95% over 12–18 months as engineers identify and eliminate the defect mechanisms causing failures. Understanding yield models is essential for understanding semiconductor economics.

Murphy's yield model (most commonly used): Y = [1 - e^(-D₀·A) / (D₀·A)]² Where: Y = yield (fraction of good dies) D₀ = defect density (defects per cm²) A = die area (cm²) Simplified Poisson model: Y ≈ e^(-D₀·A) Key insight: yield decreases exponentially with die area. A 2× larger die at the same defect density has ~Y² yield. Example: D₀ = 0.1 defects/cm², A = 5 cm² (large GPU die) Y ≈ e^(-0.1 × 5) = e^(-0.5) ≈ 61% At D₀ = 0.1, A = 0.5 cm² (small embedded CPU): Y ≈ e^(-0.05) ≈ 95%

This exponential relationship between die area and yield is why chiplet architectures — breaking one large die into multiple smaller dies — are so compelling economically. A monolithic GPU die of 800mm² at 70% yield costs roughly the same to produce as 1/(0.7) = 1.43 wafer "equivalents." Splitting the same functionality into four 200mm² chiplets, each yielding 92%, costs 4 × (1/0.92) = 4.35 chiplet-equivalents — fewer waste chips, lower total cost, even accounting for packaging overhead.

Case Study — Intel Meteor Lake and the Chiplet Gamble

Intel's Meteor Lake (launched late 2023) was the company's first consumer processor using chiplet integration at scale. Rather than one monolithic die, Meteor Lake consists of four tiles — a CPU compute tile on Intel 4 process, a GPU tile on TSMC N5, an SoC tile on TSMC N6, and an I/O tile on Intel 16 — all connected through a package-level fabric called Foveros (face-to-face die stacking with through-silicon vias).

The strategic logic: Intel could place its most sensitive, hardest-to-yield components (CPU cores, in which Intel has process expertise) on its own fab, while outsourcing the GPU and SoC tiles to TSMC where those workloads yield better. This is yield optimisation at an architectural level — matching each workload to the process where it achieves the best economics.

The execution challenge: Chiplet integration introduces new failure modes — die-to-die interconnect opens and shorts, inter-tile signalling timing, and thermal coupling between tiles running different workloads. Meteor Lake's initial reviews showed power consumption higher than expected in some workloads, partly attributed to the communication overhead of tiles running on different voltage domains. The theoretical yield benefit of chiplets only materialises if the assembly yield is high enough to offset the added complexity.

The broader lesson: Chiplet integration is not primarily a technology problem — it is a standards and ecosystem problem. For chiplets from different manufacturers to interoperate, they need common interface specifications (UCIe, HBI), common package substrates (TSMC's CoWoS, Intel's EMIB), and tested design methodologies. The industry is in the early stages of this standardisation, and the tooling, testing, and design methodology infrastructure is still maturing.

Focus Areas for Future Study

  • EUV economics and ASML's supply chain: The geopolitics of EUV — US export restrictions, the Dutch government's involvement, China's inability to access EUV — is the most consequential technology trade dispute since the Toshiba machine tool case (1987). Understanding the supply chain deeply is prerequisite for analysing semiconductor geopolitics.
  • Ruthenium and molybdenum interconnects: Copper's resistivity increases dramatically below ~10nm wire width due to grain boundary and surface scattering. Ruthenium (Ru) and molybdenum (Mo) have better size-scaling properties and are being introduced in sub-2nm BEOL layers. This is an active research area with significant near-term commercial impact.
  • Advanced packaging (UCIe, EMIB, Foveros, SoIC): The standards and economics of die-to-die integration are becoming as important as the transistor node itself. UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express) as an open standard is the key technology to watch.

The Memory Wall

There is a fundamental asymmetry built into the history of computing. Processor performance improved at roughly 60% per year through the 1980s and 1990s. Memory bandwidth improved at roughly 10% per year over the same period. By 1995, this gap had grown large enough that William Wulf and Sally McKee published a paper calling it "Hitting the Memory Wall" — warning that compute improvements would increasingly go to waste waiting for data from memory. Thirty years later, the wall is taller than ever, and it is the defining architectural constraint of artificial intelligence at scale.

The Von Neumann Bottleneck

The von Neumann architecture — the foundation of virtually every computer built since 1945 — separates storage (memory) from computation (processor) and requires data to flow between them over a bus. Every time the processor needs data, it must fetch it from memory across this bus. In modern systems, this bus is the fundamental performance bottleneck for any workload that accesses more data than fits in the processor's on-chip cache.

The severity of this bottleneck for any particular workload is characterised by its arithmetic intensity — the ratio of floating-point operations performed to bytes of data moved. A workload that does 1,000 operations on 8 bytes of data (intensity = 125 FLOP/byte) will be compute-bound; most of its time is spent in the arithmetic units. A workload that does 8 operations on 1,000 bytes (intensity = 0.008 FLOP/byte) will be memory-bound; most of its time is spent waiting for data to arrive from memory.

DRAM — Physics of the Refresh Cycle

Dynamic RAM stores each bit as charge on a capacitor paired with a single access transistor (the 1T1C cell). The "dynamic" in DRAM refers to its fatal flaw: the charge leaks. Every cell must be read and rewritten — refreshed — at least every 64 milliseconds to prevent data loss. This is not a manufacturing defect; it is a fundamental consequence of physics. The leakage current through the access transistor's off-state and through the capacitor dielectric itself is small but nonzero, and at the charge levels involved (~femtocoulombs on a ~10–22 fF capacitor), it matters.

The refresh requirement has profound implications. During a refresh operation, a memory row is activated (all cells in a row read out to sense amplifiers) then written back, taking ~50–100ns. A 16Gbit DRAM with 64K rows must refresh every 64ms, meaning one row every 64ms/65536 ≈ 1µs. The fraction of time spent refreshing rather than serving read/write requests — the refresh overhead — is typically 3–5% but becomes increasingly problematic as DRAM capacity increases and the number of rows grows faster than the allowable refresh rate.

Case Study — SK Hynix's HBM Market Position

As of 2024, SK Hynix supplies the majority of HBM for NVIDIA's H100 and H200 accelerators. This was not inevitable — Samsung held the dominant DRAM position overall, and Micron was a serious competitor. How did SK Hynix come to dominate the most valuable segment of the memory market?

The technical bet: SK Hynix invested heavily in TSV process development earlier than its competitors, accepting lower yields at smaller scale to accumulate manufacturing learning. Each generation of HBM requires solving new TSV aspect ratio, microbump bonding, and underfill challenges — and the company that has solved the previous generation has a significant head start on the next. SK Hynix qualified HBM2E for NVIDIA's A100 (2020), giving it the learning curve advantage entering HBM3 development.

The qualification bottleneck: NVIDIA does not simply buy the best available HBM from any supplier. Qualifying a new memory supplier for a specific GPU is a 12–18 month process of co-design, signal integrity characterisation, thermal testing, and system-level validation. This "sticky" qualification process means the HBM supply relationship tends to persist across generations — switching suppliers requires requalifying the entire system.

Micron's catch-up: Micron was notably absent from the initial HBM3 supply for H100. The company's aggressive investment in HBM3E production capacity (announced 2023–2024) represents an attempt to break into the qualification cycle for the next generation. Whether it succeeds will be a major determinant of memory industry economics through the late 2020s.

Focus Areas for Future Study

  • Processing-in-Memory (PIM) and near-memory computing: The ultimate response to the memory wall — moving compute into or adjacent to the DRAM itself to eliminate data movement. Samsung Aquabolt-XL and SK Hynix AiM add simple processing elements inside HBM stacks. Academic research on true in-DRAM computing (UPMEM, Mnemosyne) is advancing. This is the most consequential open research area in the memory-compute interface.
  • SRAM scaling limits: The 6T SRAM cell (six transistors per bit) is scaling poorly below 5nm. The industry is exploring alternatives: MRAM (Magnetic RAM), FeRAM (Ferroelectric RAM), and SRAM with assist circuits. On-chip cache capacity growth has stalled, exacerbating the memory wall for CPU workloads.
  • 3D NAND and Storage Class Memory: Intel's Optane (3D XPoint) attempted to fill the latency gap between DRAM and flash — it failed commercially but taught the industry important lessons about tier management. Understanding why Optane failed despite compelling specs is a useful case study in the gap between technical merit and commercial success.
Chapter 05
Theory + Pathway

HBM — The Bridge

High Bandwidth Memory is what happens when the memory wall collides with a workload that will pay any price to break through it. The physics of HBM is not new — stacking dies with through-silicon vias was demonstrated in research labs in the 1990s. What changed in 2015, when AMD and SK Hynix shipped the first commercial HBM product in the Radeon R9 Fury, was the arrival of a workload — graphics, and soon after, deep learning — that made the economics of this expensive manufacturing approach justifiable at scale.

The Bandwidth Equation

Memory bandwidth is the product of three factors: bus width (how many bits transfer in parallel), clock frequency (how many times per second the bus transfers), and efficiency (what fraction of theoretical peak the system actually achieves):

Memory bandwidth = (Bus width × Clock frequency × Transfers per clock) × Efficiency DDR5-6400 (modern system DRAM): Bus width: 64 bits (one DIMM) Clock: 3200 MHz base (6400 MT/s double data rate) Bandwidth: 64 × 6400 × 10⁶ / 8 = 51.2 GB/s per DIMM With 2 DIMMs (dual-channel): 102.4 GB/s HBM3 (one stack, 8 DRAM dies): Bus width: 1024 bits (8 channels × 128 bits) Clock: 3200 MHz (6400 MT/s DDR) Bandwidth: 1024 × 6400 × 10⁶ / 8 = 819 GB/s per stack H100 with 6 stacks: ~4.9 TB/s theoretical The secret: 1024-bit bus width vs 64-bit DDR5. This 16× wider bus is only possible because HBM's bus travels ~50µm (die-to-die), not ~100mm (PCB trace to DIMM slot). Physical distance × frequency × capacitance = power. Short distance = low power = wide bus economically feasible.

Why the Wide Bus Is Only Possible Through Stacking

The fundamental reason HBM can have a 1024-bit bus while DDR5 is stuck at 64-bit comes down to physics and economics of electrical signalling. Every signal trace has capacitance, and charging that capacitance requires energy. A DDR5 trace from an SO-DIMM slot to the CPU is ~80–100mm long; at DDR5's 6.4 Gbps signalling rate, driving this trace consumes roughly 3–5 mW per pin. A 1024-bit DDR5 bus would consume 3–5 watts just in signalling — before any memory access power — and would require 1024 pins on the CPU package, which is physically impractical.

HBM's microbump trace from the logic die to the interposer RDL is ~50–100µm — roughly 1,000× shorter. The capacitance is ~100× lower. Signalling power per pin drops proportionally. A 1024-bit HBM3 bus consumes roughly 10–15W total for all six stacks in an H100 — comparable to what 64-bit DDR5 would consume. Width buys bandwidth for free, once the physical distance is short enough.

The Full Pathway: From Silicon Physics to AI System

Band theory establishes that silicon can be doped to create controllable pn junctions. Carrier mobility determines how fast charges move. The 1.12 eV bandgap gives silicon stability at operating temperatures.
2
The DRAM cell
The 1T1C cell stores one bit as charge on a ~20 fF capacitor. The leakage physics forces the refresh requirement. Cell density is maximised by scaling the transistor (shorter L) and the capacitor (deeper trench or taller stack).

Individual dies are thinned to 50µm, tested as Known-Good Die, then stacked 8–12 high using thermal compression bonding at 250–280°C. TSV tips revealed on die backside connect to the die above via microbumps.
5

HBM stacks and the GPU die are placed on a silicon interposer (~800mm²) with RDL routing at 0.4–2µm pitch, connecting the 1024-bit HBM bus to the GPU die across distances impossible on organic substrates.
6

The assembled package delivers 3–8 TB/s of memory bandwidth to thousands of compute cores. Tensor cores perform matrix multiplication on data streamed from HBM. LLM inference becomes a race to serve weights from HBM to compute fast enough.
7
Cluster & AI system
Thousands of GPU+HBM nodes linked by NVLink and InfiniBand become the substrate for training 100B+ parameter models. The memory hierarchy — HBM → NVLink bandwidth → InfiniBand → CPU DRAM — determines achievable model size and training speed.
Case Study — How HBM3 Defined the H100's Market Position

When NVIDIA launched the H100 in 2022, its headline specifications were 3.35 TB/s HBM3 bandwidth and 80 GB capacity — numbers that made every competing product obsolete for LLM workloads at a stroke. AMD's competing MI250X used HBM2e at 3.2 TB/s, close on bandwidth; but AMD's software ecosystem (ROCm vs CUDA) was materially less mature. Google's TPUv4 used HBM2 at 1.2 TB/s per chip — far below the H100.

The H100's dominance in the AI training market through 2023–2024 was not primarily due to its tensor core performance (AMD's numbers were competitive on paper). It was due to the combination of HBM3 bandwidth enabling larger batch sizes, CUDA's ecosystem lock-in, and NVLink's superior multi-GPU communication — all of which compounded. An AI lab could train a 70B parameter model on 8× H100s with sufficient HBM capacity; the same model on AMD's hardware required more complex software engineering to achieve comparable throughput.

The H200 response: NVIDIA's H200 (2024) was essentially an H100 with HBM3e instead of HBM3 — 141 GB at 4.8 TB/s vs 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s. The memory upgrade alone, without changing the compute architecture, made the H200 dramatically better for inference of large models. A 70B model fits on one H200 in FP16; two H100s were previously required. This cut the inference cost per token roughly in half for models of that size. Memory capacity, not compute, was the binding constraint — and NVIDIA solved it by going back to the HBM manufacturer (SK Hynix) for a denser, faster stack.

Focus Areas for Future Study

  • HBM4 and hybrid bonding: The transition from microbump bonding to Cu-Cu direct hybrid bonding (eliminating solder entirely) enables <10µm bump pitch, 4× more interconnects, and zero bump height. Understanding the surface preparation and bonding physics (Van der Waals forces, Cu interdiffusion) is the key frontier in advanced packaging.
  • HBM yield economics: The stacking yield model (Y_stack = Y_die^N) creates a powerful incentive to improve individual die yield. Even 1% improvement in DRAM die yield translates to ~8% improvement in stack yield for an 8-die stack. Analysing SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron earnings reports through this lens reveals the strategic importance of yield engineering investment.
  • CXL (Compute Express Link) memory: CXL 3.x enables memory pooling and memory expansion over a PCIe-compatible protocol. While not a replacement for HBM bandwidth, CXL allows disaggregated memory architectures where HBM serves as a fast cache and CXL-attached DRAM provides cheaper capacity expansion. This is an emerging area for large inference deployments.

Theory + Case Study

AI Hardware Architecture

The GPU did not begin as an AI accelerator. It began as a specialised processor for rendering triangles — applying lighting models, texture lookups, and rasterisation to the geometric descriptions of 3D scenes. The reason GPUs became the dominant platform for AI training is not coincidence; it is a deep structural alignment between the mathematics of graphics and the mathematics of machine learning, revealed by researchers at a moment when GPU hardware had just crossed a threshold of programmability.

Why Matrix Multiplication Maps to Silicon

The dominant operation in both graphics shading and neural network inference is the matrix multiplication: C = A × B, where A, B, and C are large two-dimensional arrays of numbers. To compute one element of C, you take the dot product of one row of A with one column of B — multiply corresponding elements and sum the results. Every element of C is independent of every other; they can all be computed simultaneously.

This data parallelism is GPU hardware's natural operating mode. A GPU with 10,000 compute cores can compute 10,000 independent matrix elements per cycle. A CPU with 16 cores doing the same matrix multiply must process elements sequentially, 16 at a time. For small matrices this doesn't matter; for the weight matrices in large neural networks (often 4096×4096 or larger), the difference is thousands-fold.

Tensor cores, introduced in NVIDIA's Volta architecture (2017), took this specialisation further. Rather than computing individual multiplications, a tensor core computes a complete 4×4×4 matrix multiply-accumulate (MMA) operation in a single cycle — taking two 4×4 matrices, multiplying them, and adding the result to a third 4×4 accumulator matrix. The H100 has 528 tensor cores, each performing one 16×16×16 MMA operation per cycle in FP16/BF16, delivering 989 TFLOPS — a number that would have been absurd to contemplate for a processor in 2017.

The SRAM vs HBM Tension in AI Compute

Modern AI accelerators have two levels of on-chip memory working simultaneously:

  • SRAM (L2 cache / shared memory): The H100 has 50 MB of L2 cache — fast (multiple TB/s), low latency (~5ns), but extremely expensive to build and limited in size. It is the "working memory" of the compute units, holding the small tiles of matrices currently being processed.
  • HBM: 80–192 GB, slower (~100ns), but far larger. It is the "main memory" holding all model weights and activations that don't currently fit in SRAM.

Flash Attention — the algorithmic innovation by Tri Dao et al. (2022) that made long-context transformers practical — is fundamentally an HBM-awareness optimization. The standard attention algorithm requires reading and writing attention matrices that are quadratic in sequence length to HBM, creating enormous memory traffic. Flash Attention restructures the computation to keep attention tiles entirely within SRAM, reducing HBM reads/writes by 5–20× for typical sequence lengths. It is one of the most important algorithmic advances in AI, and it is entirely motivated by semiconductor memory hierarchy physics.

Google's TPU and the Systolic Array Architecture

Google's Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) took a different architectural approach from NVIDIA's GPU. Rather than a general-purpose massively parallel processor adapted for matrix multiplication, the TPU was designed from scratch around a systolic array — a 2D grid of simple multiply-accumulate units where data flows rhythmically through the grid in one direction (like blood through a heart — hence "systolic") while partial sums accumulate in the perpendicular direction.

The systolic array approach has several advantages: it maximises arithmetic intensity by reusing each piece of data multiple times as it flows through the array, it has no cache hierarchy (data flows predictably, so no cache is needed), and its regular structure is highly efficient to fabricate and verify. TPUv4 achieves 275 TFLOPS at 180W — lower peak than H100's 989 TFLOPS, but with dramatically better power efficiency for the specific matrix multiplication patterns of transformer inference.

Case Study — AMD MI300X and the Unified Memory Strategy

AMD's Instinct MI300X (2024) took a different architectural gamble than NVIDIA: instead of separating the GPU compute die from the HBM (as NVIDIA does with an interposer), AMD stacked the GPU chiplets and DRAM dies into a single package with 192 GB of HBM3 — 2.4× more than the H100's 80 GB.

The strategic logic: at 192 GB, the MI300X can hold a 70B parameter model in FP16 on a single card. The H100 requires two cards. For inference deployments where serving one large model on minimal hardware is the priority, the MI300X's memory capacity advantage translates directly to lower cost per inference.

The weakness: AMD's ROCm software stack, while significantly improved in 2023–2024, still lags CUDA's ecosystem depth for training. Most cutting-edge AI research is developed on CUDA-first, with ROCm compatibility added later if at all. The MI300X is winning inference deployments (Microsoft Azure, Meta) but has not displaced H100/H200 for training at leading AI labs.

The broader lesson: Memory capacity and memory bandwidth are now primary dimensions of GPU competition, alongside raw compute. NVIDIA's response to the MI300X was the H200 — same compute, more HBM. The battle for the inference market is being fought on the memory spec sheet.

Focus Areas for Future Study

  • Sparse computation and structured sparsity: Most neural network weights are close to zero. Hardware that skips multiplications by zero (sparse tensor cores) can deliver 2× effective throughput. Understanding sparsity representations (unstructured, block-sparse, MX formats) is increasingly important as models are pruned for deployment.
  • FP8 and lower-precision training: H100 supports FP8 (8-bit floating point) for training — not just inference. The numerical stability implications of training at this precision are an active research area. Transformer Engine (NVIDIA) handles the precision management automatically; understanding how is increasingly important for infrastructure engineers.
  • Custom silicon (Groq, Cerebras, SambaNova): Startups building AI-specific chips that take the "fixed dataflow" principle further than NVIDIA's GPU architecture. Groq's LPU (Language Processing Unit) is a systolic array optimised for inference latency; Cerebras builds wafer-scale processors that eliminate HBM entirely. Evaluating these architectures requires understanding the memory-compute trade-offs discussed in Chapters 4–5.

HPC — The Older Sibling

High-Performance Computing (HPC) predates the AI hardware boom by fifty years. Its goal — using arrays of processors to solve problems too large or too slow for a single machine — is identical in principle to large-scale AI training. Yet HPC and AI emerged from different scientific traditions, developed different software ecosystems, and optimised their hardware for subtly different properties. Understanding the relationship between them reveals where they are converging, where they differ fundamentally, and what each community can learn from the other.

What HPC Is — and How It Differs from AI

HPC workloads are typically characterised by tight numerical coupling: the output of one compute node's calculation is input to another's, and those dependencies flow across the entire problem domain at every timestep. A fluid dynamics simulation computing the airflow over a wing must exchange pressure and velocity values at every shared boundary between adjacent spatial subdomains — thousands of times per second across hundreds of nodes. This makes latency (the time for one node's message to reach another) as important as bandwidth.

AI training, by contrast, is characterised by relatively coarse synchronisation points. In the dominant training paradigm (data-parallel training with gradient synchronisation), each GPU runs its local computation independently for a full forward+backward pass before synchronising gradients with all other GPUs. The synchronisation is an all-reduce collective — expensive in bandwidth but tolerant of tens of microseconds of latency because the compute phase lasts milliseconds. AI favours bandwidth; classical HPC favours latency.

The FP64 requirement is perhaps the most consequential difference. Scientific simulations — climate models, quantum chemistry, molecular dynamics — require double-precision floating point because errors accumulate over millions of timesteps, and single-precision rounding can cause physically nonsensical results. NVIDIA's H100 delivers 989 TFLOPS in FP16 but only 67 TFLOPS in FP64 — a 15× difference. GPU vendors structurally deprioritise FP64 because AI (a far larger market) doesn't need it.

Supercomputer Architecture — Frontier, Aurora, LUMI

The world's fastest computers as of 2024 are not monolithic machines but tightly orchestrated networks of GPU-equipped nodes. Frontier (Oak Ridge National Laboratory, 2022) was the first exascale computer — capable of 10¹⁸ floating-point operations per second. It uses 74,000 AMD EPYC CPUs and 37,000 AMD Radeon Instinct MI250X GPUs, connected by a custom HPE Cray Slingshot 11 network with 25 Gb/s per link and full all-to-all connectivity within 64-node "dragonfly" groups.

Case Study — Why Frontier Chose AMD Over NVIDIA

The US Department of Energy awarded the Frontier contract to Cray/HPE with AMD CPUs and GPUs in 2019. At that time, NVIDIA's A100 was demonstrably the most powerful GPU on the market. Why AMD?

FP64 performance: The AMD MI200 series delivers competitive FP64 throughput relative to its TFLOPS rating — AMD did not deprioritise FP64 as aggressively as NVIDIA. For scientific HPC, this matters enormously. NVIDIA's A100 delivers 312 TFLOPS FP16 but only 19.5 TFLOPS FP64; AMD MI250X delivers 383 TFLOPS FP16 and 95.7 TFLOPS FP64 — nearly 5× NVIDIA's FP64 per card.

Unified CPU-GPU memory: AMD's Infinity Fabric links the EPYC CPUs and MI GPUs in Frontier nodes with cache-coherent shared memory access. This means the GPU can directly access CPU DRAM and vice versa without explicit data transfer management — enormously simplifying certain HPC programming patterns where problem decomposition naturally spans CPU and GPU memories.

US government CHIPS considerations: The Exascale Computing Project was partly a national security initiative — the ability to simulate nuclear stockpile reliability without physical testing requires exascale compute. Using a domestic US chipmaker (AMD) rather than a company (NVIDIA) with significant operations in Taiwan carried policy weight in the procurement decision.

The AI-HPC Convergence

The wall between AI and HPC is dissolving, for reasons that run in both directions. AI methods — specifically large neural networks trained on simulated data — are replacing traditional numerical solvers in several HPC domains. DeepMind's AlphaFold 2 solved protein structure prediction, a 50-year grand challenge problem, using a neural network trained on known structures rather than physics simulation. NVIDIA's FourCastNet and Google's GraphCast run global weather forecasts in seconds rather than hours by replacing partial differential equation solvers with transformer models trained on decades of reanalysis data.

In the other direction, HPC infrastructure — job schedulers (SLURM), parallel file systems (Lustre), high-speed interconnects (InfiniBand), and the culture of careful performance profiling and optimisation — is being adopted wholesale by AI training centres. NVIDIA's NeMo and Megatron-LM training frameworks are essentially MPI programs adapted for GPU-specific collective communication patterns.

Focus Areas for Future Study

  • MPI and distributed computing fundamentals: Even if you primarily work with PyTorch/JAX, understanding MPI collective operations (allreduce, allgather, scatter) and their implementation in InfiniBand networks explains the behaviour of NCCL (NVIDIA's collective communication library) and the performance characteristics of large training runs.
  • Quantum computing — genuinely disruptive or perpetually 10 years away? Quantum computers solve certain problems (prime factorisation via Shor's algorithm, quantum chemistry via variational algorithms) with theoretical exponential speedups over classical approaches. The honest assessment: current quantum hardware is too noisy to demonstrate practical advantage for commercially relevant problem sizes. The timeline for fault-tolerant quantum computers capable of running Shor's algorithm at cryptographically relevant scales is 10–20+ years. For HPC simulation applications, quantum advantage for specific chemistry and optimisation problems may arrive in the 2030s. Do not let hype substitute for understanding the error rate requirements — a quantum circuit must maintain coherence for millions of gate operations, and current devices barely manage thousands.
  • Neuromorphic computing: Intel's Loihi 2 and IBM's NorthPole process information as event-driven spikes rather than synchronised floating-point operations, mimicking biological neural computation. Power efficiency for sparse, asynchronous workloads is the claimed advantage. Genuinely interesting physics, genuinely uncertain commercial trajectory.

Critical Analysis

Where This Field Is Going — An Honest Assessment

Having traced the pathway from electron band structure to transformer training clusters, it is worth standing back and asking what we know, what we don't know, and where the honest intellectual challenges lie. The semiconductor and AI industries are simultaneously among the most technically sophisticated and most hype-prone fields in engineering. Learning to distinguish genuine advances from marketing noise is itself a critical competence.

The End of Classical Scaling

The industry consensus, stated plainly, is that we are in the late stages of traditional transistor scaling. FinFET to GAA was a significant improvement; GAA to CFET (Complementary FET, stacking n and p nanosheets vertically) will provide another node's worth of density improvement. Beyond that — at sub-1nm effective channel lengths — quantum mechanical effects (tunnelling from source to drain, discrete dopant atoms as percentage of total atoms) make the classical MOSFET concept increasingly incoherent as a physical model.

This does not mean compute capacity stops growing. The industry has four remaining levers beyond classical scaling:

  • 3D integration: Stacking compute dies vertically (as HBM stacks memory dies) to reduce interconnect length and increase effective density without shrinking individual devices.
  • Advanced packaging: Disaggregating systems into chiplets optimised for their specific workload, assembled on advanced interposers with near-die-level interconnect density.
  • New materials: 2D channel materials, high-mobility compound semiconductor channels, ferroelectric memory integration — all real, none yet deployed at scale.
  • Specialisation: Purpose-built hardware (TPUs, LPUs, custom inference chips) that achieves better efficiency on specific workloads by sacrificing generality.

The Geopolitics of Silicon

TSMC manufactures approximately 90% of the world's most advanced logic chips (sub-5nm) in Taiwan — an island over which the People's Republic of China claims sovereignty and which the People's Liberation Army regularly conducts military exercises involving blockade simulations. The concentration of the global technology supply chain in a geopolitically contested region is without historical precedent in its scale and strategic significance.

The CHIPS Act (US, 2022), the European Chips Act (EU, 2023), Japan's semiconductor revival programme, and India's Semicon India initiative all represent attempts to diversify manufacturing geography. The honest assessment of their likely impact through 2030: TSMC's Arizona fabs (N4, N3) will produce meaningful volume of leading-edge chips in the US, but the full stack of cleanroom expertise, supplier ecosystem, and process knowledge that TSMC has built in Taiwan over 35 years cannot be replicated in 5–10 years regardless of capital investment. Geographic diversification of manufacturing is genuinely happening; geographic parity in capability is a generation away at minimum.

Critical Assessment — Claims to Evaluate Sceptically

"Process node X nm" — transistor gate length has not equalled the node name since approximately Intel 90nm (2004). TSMC's "3nm" N3 has gate length ~12nm. The node name is a marketing convention; compare transistor density (MTx/mm²) or specific circuit benchmarks instead.

"Quantum supremacy/advantage" — Google's 2019 quantum supremacy demonstration solved a problem specifically chosen to be easy for quantum hardware and hard for classical. No commercially relevant problem has demonstrated quantum advantage on real hardware. Evaluate by: error rates per gate (<0.001% needed for fault tolerance, current hardware: ~1%), qubit count (millions needed for Shor's at scale, current: thousands), and coherence time.

"X TFLOPS" AI performance — compare metrics at the same precision (FP16 vs BF16 vs FP8 vs INT8 differ by 2–4×), the same sparsity assumption (2× sparsity is standard in NVIDIA's marketing), and verify real-world benchmark performance rather than theoretical peak.

The Energy Problem — Not a Side Issue

Training GPT-4 consumed an estimated 50 GWh of electricity — equivalent to the annual power consumption of about 5,000 US households. Training GPT-5 and similar frontier models likely requires 10–100× more. Data centres already consume roughly 1–2% of global electricity; AI workloads are the fastest-growing component of that consumption.

The semiconductor industry's power consumption is not primarily an environmental ethics question — it is an engineering constraint. The fundamental limit on AI cluster size is not silicon availability or DRAM manufacturing capacity; it is the ability to build and power cooling infrastructure. Each megawatt of AI compute requires 1–2 MW of cooling infrastructure (at PUE 1.5–2.0) and 3–5 acres of land for a dedicated power substation. The transition to direct liquid cooling (DLC) and immersion cooling reduces PUE toward 1.05–1.1, but does not reduce the fundamental compute-to-power ratio.

Honest Gaps in Current Knowledge (Across the Field)

These are areas where the field does not have good answers — worth knowing so you calibrate confidence appropriately:

  • Post-GAA transistor physics at <1nm effective length: Source-to-drain tunnelling rates, dopant atomistic variability, and gate dielectric breakdown mechanisms in quantum confinement regimes are active research with significant uncertainty.
  • Hybrid bonding yield at <5µm pitch: The surface preparation and Cu-Cu interdiffusion physics at this pitch are not fully characterised at production scale. HBM4's yield learning curve is genuinely unknown.
  • AI compute scaling laws: The Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) describe compute-optimal training; they do not predict whether performance continues scaling indefinitely or reaches a plateau. The question of whether frontier model capabilities plateau with compute scaling is an open empirical question with enormous economic implications.
  • Long-term HBM error rates: Large-scale deployment of HBM in AI clusters is a recent phenomenon. The long-term field failure rates, wear-out mechanisms, and effective lifetime under sustained high-bandwidth loads are not yet characterised across the full device lifetime. Published data from hyperscalers on this topic is sparse.
